Evidence on duration of protection and waning after **hexavalent vaccination** (DTaP/DTwP–IPV–HepB–Hib) is largely derived from serological persistence studies and longer-term population serosurveys of individual antigen components, with additional data in selected risk groups.

- **Diphtheria component**
  - **Waning after primary/early booster schedules:** In Thai children primed in infancy and boosted at 18 months, anti-diphtheria IgG declined between 19 and 48 months of age (pre-second booster) [12]. At 48 months (pre-second booster), seroprotection (anti-DT IgG ≥0.1 IU/mL) was 84.2% in the hexavalent (aP) group (vs 71.8% pentavalent wP; 76.8% EPI wP) [12]. After a second booster at 4 years, seroprotection was 100% at 49 months and remained 91% at 72 months (2 years post-second booster) in the hexavalent group [12].
  - **Longer-term persistence with multi-dose schedules:** In the Netherlands, a schedule comprising a complete 3-dose primary series plus 3 booster doses prior to adolescence was associated with very high seroprevalence above the basic protection threshold (≥0.01 IU/mL) up to 39 years of age and potentially longer; in adults aged 35–39 years, 94.6% (95% CI 87.3–100%) remained ≥0.01 IU/mL, while 37.8% (95% CI 22.2–53.5%) were ≥0.1 IU/mL (full protection threshold) [1]. These data were interpreted as suggesting decennial boosters may not be necessary through middle age under this schedule [1].
  - **Time since last dose and immune memory:** Among Portuguese women vaccinated in childhood with a complete DTP-containing primary series and ≥1 booster (n=22), none were susceptible before 25 years had elapsed since the last dose [1]. Those with ≥6 doses (n=17) had antibody levels above the full protection threshold up to 38 years since the last dose [1]. A US cross-sectional seroprevalence study modelled a half-life for diphtheria-specific immunity (>0.01 IU/mL) of 27 years (95% CI 18–51) [1]. Booster responses can still be elicited after 25–30 years, supporting persistence of immunological memory even when boosters are delayed [1].
  - **Risk groups (HIV):** HIV-1 infected children had lower serological responses after infant diphtheria toxoid vaccination (protective titres in 70.8% vs 98.5% in HIV-negative children; P<0.05) and lower geometric mean titres [1]. HIV-1 infected women had lower diphtheria antibody levels than uninfected women, including after booster vaccination [1].

- **Tetanus component**
  - **Waning and duration after early-life schedules:** Serological evidence summarized across studies suggests that a 3-dose tetanus-toxoid-containing vaccine (TTCV) primary series in infancy plus a booster in the second year of life provides approximately 3–5 years of protection [2, 8]. Immunity is described as being boosted after each subsequent dose and then waning over time [8]. One synthesis noted that as early as 1 year after vaccination, up to 20% may be poorly protected and mean titres may fall to the protective threshold [2].
  - **Persistence in childhood cohorts including hexavalent schedules:** In Thai children followed from 2 to 6 years of age, anti-tetanus IgG decreased from month 19 to month 48, but seroprotection (anti-TT IgG ≥0.1 IU/mL) remained 99.2% at 48 months pre-second booster in the hexavalent group and 100% in comparator groups [12]. After the second booster at 4 years, tetanus seroprotection was 100% at 49, 60, and 72 months in all groups [12].
  - **Longer-term persistence with additional doses and immune memory:** Summarized evidence indicates immunity may persist ~10 years after a fourth TTCV dose and at least 20 years after a fifth dose [8]. In the Netherlands, a 6-dose childhood schedule (last dose at 8 years) showed tetanus immunity persisting at least 20 years after the sixth dose, with GMT 0.44 IU/mL in adults aged 30–34 years; regression modelling predicted protective levels could persist until 90 years of age (GMT 0.22 IU/mL) [2]. Booster responses can still be elicited after long intervals (20–30 years or 25–30 years), indicating durable immunological memory [2, 8].
  - **Adults/older age as a risk group:** Evidence syntheses note that in settings without adult boosters or with low uptake, a proportion of older adults may have significant waning to below putatively protective thresholds [8]. Immunity gaps in the elderly are also described, attributed to waning or non-vaccination, with particular concern among elderly females in some contexts [2].
  - **Risk groups (HIV):** Among HIV-positive children and adults, the proportion responding to TTCVs is described as similar to HIV-uninfected individuals, but responses are often lower and may wane more quickly [8].

- **Pertussis component (acellular or whole-cell priming within combination schedules)**
  - **Waning after vaccination:** Reviews and epidemiologic data indicate vaccine-derived protection wanes, increasing susceptibility in adolescents and adults, particularly with reduced natural boosting [3]. Duration of protective immunity after pertussis vaccination is commonly estimated at 4–12 years [3, 7].
  - **Whole-cell pertussis (wP) effectiveness over time:** In a UK general practice community, wP vaccine effectiveness declined from 100% in the first year after primary vaccination to 84% in year 4, 52% in year 5, and 46% in year 6 [3]. Additional summarized evidence indicates that 95% of pertussis attacks occurred within 12 years since the last wP dose, and one estimate suggested only 52% of children retained clinically protective immunity by 4 years post-immunization [7].
  - **Acellular pertussis (aP) persistence and programmatic implications:** In Italy, two aP-containing vaccines showed protective efficacies of 76% and 85% at 6 years after completion of a 2,4,6-month primary series (using two clinical case definitions) [3]. In Sweden, a 2-dose primary aP series with a booster at 12 months provided protection for approximately 5 years [3]. Evidence syntheses note increasing evidence that protection after aP boosters wanes faster in individuals primed with aP rather than wP, and that in low-incidence settings a 3-dose primary series plus a booster in the second year may be insufficient beyond age >6 years, supporting an additional booster at school entry [3]. In a Thai persistence study including hexavalent recipients, anti-pertussis toxin (anti-PT) GMCs decreased over time at 24, 36, and 48 months [12].

- **Hepatitis B component**
  - **Long-term protection despite antibody waning:** A long-term cohort in Alaska (3-dose schedule at 0,1,6 months) prevented all clinically apparent and chronic HBV infection for at least 30 years [4]. In the same cohort, among primary responders without boosters, 49% had anti-HBs <10 mIU/mL at 30 years, yet most (88%) mounted a rapid anamnestic response to a booster, supporting persistence of immunologic memory [4]. Protection was estimated at ~90% (range 74–100%) for at least 30 years irrespective of measurable anti-HBs [4].
  - **30-year cohort details on waning and booster response:** In the 30-year follow-up, 51% (125/243) of primary responders without subsequent doses had anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL at 30 years, and among those with anti-HBs <10 mIU/mL, 88% (75/85) responded to a booster with anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL at 30 days [10]. Overall, the study estimated 94% had evidence of protection at 30 years (anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL and/or booster response), with no new breakthrough infections observed at 30 years [10]. However, antibody levels after a booster can wane substantially: 8 years after a booster given at 22 years, only 14% (9/63) had anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL (GMC 3.6 mIU/mL) [10].
  - **Hexavalent-specific persistence in childhood:** In Thai children receiving hexavalent (Infanrix hexa) with a booster at 18 months, anti-HBs waned over time but remained ≥10 mIU/mL in 95.8% at age 3 years, 95.0% at 4 years, 92.5% at 5 years, 90.1% at 6 years, and 86.9% at 7 years (≈5.5 years after the last HepB dose), with consistently higher GMCs than pentavalent comparators (e.g., at 7 years: 143.2 mIU/mL [95% CI 93.4–219.6] vs 14.8 and 9.6 mIU/mL) [11]. In Indian infants vaccinated with pentavalent DTwP-HB-Hib at 6–10–14 weeks, hepatitis B seroprotection (anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL) at 15–18 months was 94% and 88% in two pentavalent groups, indicating persistence into the second year of life [14].
  - **Risk groups (HIV):** A 2014 systematic review/meta-analysis in HIV-positive individuals found higher peak anti-HBs titres with higher-dose regimens but no clear difference in the proportion with protective antibodies up to 5 years in adults, and no improvement in duration of seroprotection 2–5 years in children receiving double vs standard dose protocols [4]. Additional long-term studies were noted as needed to assess booster needs in subgroups, particularly HIV-infected/HIV-exposed infants [4].

- **Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) component**
  - **Waning of antibody levels vs clinical impact:** Field studies set anti-PRP IgG ≥1 µg/mL one month after primary series completion as a level associated with long-term protection against invasive Hib disease [5]. Although the proportion of vaccinees above antibody thresholds may decrease over time, evidence linking this decline to increased clinical disease is limited [5]. Some countries reported small increases in incidence after successful control with a 3-dose primary schedule, and in the UK a resurgence occurred after about a decade, prompting introduction of a booster dose [5]. In four South American countries, Hib meningitis rates 6–10 years after vaccine introduction were similar in settings with and without booster doses [5].
  - **Persistence in combination-vaccine cohorts:** In Thai children followed to age 3 years, anti-Hib IgG declined at 24 and 36 months, but seroprotection (≥0.15 µg/mL) remained 100% at both time points in hexavalent and pentavalent groups, with >90% achieving levels associated with long-term protection (>1.0 µg/mL) [12]. In Indian infants assessed at 15–18 months after pentavalent primary vaccination, 99–100% had anti-PRP ≥0.15 µg/mL and 76–83% had ≥1.0 µg/mL (suggesting longer-term protection) [14].
  - **Risk groups (HIV):** Limited evidence (mainly from South Africa) suggests HIV-infected children would benefit from a Hib booster dose regardless of the number of primary doses received and regardless of antiretroviral therapy [5].

- **Polio (IPV) component**
  - **Circulating antibody persistence and interpretation of waning:** Active immunity induced by vaccination or exposure is described as providing lifelong protection against paralytic disease, even though antibody titres may decline and seroprevalence may not fully reflect immune status [6]. For IPV, information from high-income countries indicates circulating antibody persists for decades and possibly for life, and persisting neutralizing antibodies are usually found in all recipients 5 years after a 3–4 dose primary series [6]. Nonetheless, antibody titres can decline such that a high proportion may become seronegative a few years after vaccination, without evidence that loss of detectable antibody places immunocompetent individuals at risk for paralytic poliomyelitis [6].
  - **Empirical seroprevalence patterns and waning:** In Sri Lanka, seropositivity for poliovirus types 1 and 2 was >95% across age groups up to 15 years, while type 3 seropositivity declined with age (95% at 9–11 months; 90% at 3–4 years; 77% at 7–9 years; 75% at 15 years) [6]. In Pakistan, poliovirus type 2 seroprevalence declined between 10 and 21 months after IPV schedules, with 86% seroprevalence at 21 months after full-dose IPV vs 67% after intradermal fractional IPV (p=0.004) [6].
  - **Risk groups (immunodeficiency):** In individuals with primary immunodeficiency disorders, OPV vaccination can lead to paralysis and persistent (>6 months to 5 years) or chronic (>5 years) shedding of iVDPV strains, indicating a distinct risk profile relevant to polio immunization strategies [6].
