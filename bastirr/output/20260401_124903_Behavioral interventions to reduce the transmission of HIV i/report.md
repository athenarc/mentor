## 4. Evidence Extraction Summary
**Study 2** — Results of a randomised trial of male condom promotion among Madagascar sex workers
- **Population:** 1000 female sex workers in Madagascar, randomized to two arms.
- **Setting:** Madagascar; peer education in community/clinic settings with clinician-delivered counselling.
- **Intervention:** Peer education supplemented with individual clinic-based risk-reduction counselling by a clinician (peer + clinic).
- **Comparator:** Condom promotion by peer educators only (peer only).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Chlamydia prevalence at 6 months: OR 0.7 (95% CI 0.4–1.0)
  - Gonorrhoea prevalence at 6 months: OR 0.7 (95% CI 0.5–1.0)
  - Trichomoniasis prevalence at 6 months: OR 0.8 (95% CI 0.6–1.2)
  - Aggregate STI prevalence at 6 months: OR 0.7 (95% CI 0.5–0.9)
  - Reported condom use with clients (past 30 days; overall across follow-up): OR 1.4 (95% CI 1.1–1.8)
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** 6 months (STI testing at baseline and 6 months; behavioral interviews at 2, 4, and 6 months)
- **Key findings:**
  - Aggregate STI prevalence increased in the peer-only arm (38.2% to 41.4%) and decreased slightly in the peer+clinic arm (34.1% to 32.1%) by 6 months.
  - Adjusted logistic regression ORs comparing peer+clinic vs peer-only: chlamydia 0.7 (95% CI 0.4–1.0), gonorrhoea 0.7 (0.5–1.0), trichomoniasis 0.8 (0.6–1.2), aggregate STI 0.7 (0.5–0.9).
  - Reported condom use OR (peer+clinic vs peer-only) rose from 1.1 at 2 months to 1.8 at 6 months; overall 1.4 (95% CI 1.1–1.8).
  - Adjustment for baseline factors changed results little.
- **Notes:** Individual randomization; objective STI outcomes alongside self-reported condom use; sample size n=1000.

**Study 3** — Protection against sexually transmitted diseases by granting sex workers in Thailand the choice from using the male or female condom: results from a randomized controlled trial
- **Population:** Sex workers in sex establishments in four cities in Thailand; results from 34 establishments (249 women) in the male/female condom group and 37 establishments (255 women) in the male-condom group.
- **Setting:** Four cities in Thailand; cluster randomization by sex establishment.
- **Intervention:** Instruction to use male condoms consistently with the option to use female condoms if clients refused or could not use male condoms.
- **Comparator:** Instruction to use male condoms consistently (male-condom only group).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Proportion of unprotected sexual acts over 24 weeks (5.9% vs 7.1%); p=0.16
  - STD incidence rate (weighted geometric mean, 2.81 vs 3.69 per 100 person-weeks); p=0.18
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** cluster RCT
- **Follow-up:** 24 weeks
- **Key findings:**
  - Overall condom use was very high in both groups (97.9% vs 97.3%; P>0.05).
  - Male condom use was lower in the male/female group (88.2% vs 97.5%; P<0.001), offset by female condom use in 12.0% of acts.
  - Unprotected acts were 17% lower in the male/female group (5.9% vs 7.1%; P=0.16).
  - STD incidence rate was 24% lower in the male/female group (2.81 vs 3.69 per 100 person-weeks; P=0.18).
  - Female condom use was sustained over the entire study period.
- **Notes:** Cluster randomization minimized cross-over (sharing of female condoms). Outcomes included direct behavior measures and lab-confirmed STDs. Effects not statistically significant.

**Study 6** — Randomised controlled trial of alternative male and female condom promotion strategies targeting sex workers in Madagascar
- **Population:** 901 sex workers attending two public dispensaries in Madagascar.
- **Setting:** Two public dispensaries in Madagascar.
- **Intervention:** Peer education supplemented with individual clinic-based counselling for male and female condom promotion.
- **Comparator:** Peer education only.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Aggregated STI prevalence
  - Reported protection (condom use) with clients
  - Reported protection (condom use) with non-paying partners
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** 12 months (clinic visits every 2 months; STI testing every 6 months)
- **Key findings:**
  - No statistically significant association between study arm and aggregated STI prevalence.
  - No substantial differences in reported protection between groups.
- **Notes:** Population had prior intensive male condom promotion exposure; objective STI testing used. No effect estimates provided in abstract.

**Study 7** — Short‐term effect of a cultural adaptation of voluntary counseling and testing among female sex workers in China: a quasi‐experimental trial
- **Population:** 400 female sex workers in Guangxi, China.
- **Setting:** Guangxi, China.
- **Intervention:** Culturally adapted voluntary counseling and testing (VCT) modeled after CDC Project RESPECT.
- **Comparator:** Standard of care: STD testing and treatment.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Consistent condom use with clients at 6 months: OR 2.23 (95% CI 1.26–3.96)
  - Any STD infection at follow-up: OR 0.44 (95% CI 0.24–0.8)
  - HIV/STD-related knowledge score; p=0.0001
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** quasi-experimental
- **Follow-up:** 6 months
- **Key findings:**
  - Intervention increased HIV/STD knowledge (p<.0001) and consistent condom use with clients (OR=2.23; 95% CI 1.26–3.96).
  - Intervention reduced STD infection rate at follow-up (OR=0.44; 95% CI 0.24–0.80).
- **Notes:** Non-randomized assignment; analyses controlled for potential confounders and baseline differences; biomarkers used for five common STDs.

**Study 8** — Efficacy of a brief behavioral intervention to promote condom use among female sex workers in Tijuana and Ciudad Juarez, Mexico
- **Population:** 924 female sex workers (≥18 years) without known HIV infection in Tijuana and Ciudad Juarez who had recently had unprotected sex with clients.
- **Setting:** Tijuana and Ciudad Juarez, Mexico.
- **Intervention:** 30-minute brief behavioral intervention.
- **Comparator:** Didactic control condition.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Cumulative STI incidence over follow-up; p=0.049
  - STI incidence density (combined) per 100 person-years (13.8 vs 24.92); p=0.034
  - HIV incidence density per 100 person-years (0 vs 2.01)
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** 6 months (interviews and testing for HIV, syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia at baseline and 6 months)
- **Key findings:**
  - 40% decline in cumulative STI incidence in the intervention group (P=0.049).
  - Combined STI incidence density: 13.8 vs 24.92 per 100 person-years (P=0.034).
  - HIV incidence density: 0 vs 2.01 per 100 person-years (P<0.001).
  - Increases in protected sex acts and decreases in unprotected sex acts with clients (P<0.05).
- **Notes:** Randomized at individual level; multiple biomarker outcomes; significant reductions in STIs and HIV incidence density.

**Study 10** — Substance use, sexual risk, and violence: HIV prevention intervention with sex workers in Pretoria
- **Population:** 93 women reporting recent substance use and sex trading in Pretoria; 80 completed the 1-month follow-up.
- **Setting:** Pretoria, South Africa.
- **Intervention:** Woman-Focused HIV prevention intervention adapted from a US program.
- **Comparator:** Modified Standard HIV intervention.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Unprotected sex (proportion reporting) at 1 month
  - Daily alcohol use
  - Daily cocaine use
  - Victimization frequency
- **Adverse events:** Violence remained prevalent; no intervention-related adverse events reported.
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** 1 month
- **Key findings:**
  - Decreases in the proportion reporting unprotected sex and in daily alcohol and cocaine use.
  - Greater reductions in daily alcohol and cocaine use among Woman-Focused participants.
  - Woman-Focused participants reported less frequent victimization at follow-up.
  - Demonstrated feasibility of a cross-cultural behavioral HIV prevention intervention.
- **Notes:** Small sample; attrition with 80/93 completing follow-up; outcomes largely self-reported; short follow-up.

**Study 12** — A randomized controlled trial of an HIV prevention intervention for street‐based female sex workers in Yerevan, Armenia: preliminary evidence of efficacy
- **Population:** 120 street-based female sex workers in Yerevan, Armenia.
- **Setting:** Yerevan, Armenia.
- **Intervention:** Single 2-hour, health educator–delivered, gender-empowerment and skills-based intervention (self-efficacy to persuade clients, condom application skills, eroticizing safer sex).
- **Comparator:** Wait-list control.
- **Outcomes:**
  - Consistent condom use with clients over 6 months; p=0.004
  - Applying condoms on clients; p=0.0001
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** 6 months (assessments at baseline, 3, and 6 months)
- **Key findings:**
  - Intervention increased consistent condom use with clients (P=0.004).
  - Intervention participants more likely to apply condoms on clients (P=0.0001).
  - Effects also observed on psychosocial mediators of safer sex.
- **Notes:** Preliminary evidence with modest sample size; self-reported behavioral outcomes.

**Study 14** — Constraints faced by sex workers in use of female and male condoms for safer sex in urban Zimbabwe
- **Population:** Sex workers in Harare, Zimbabwe; randomized to receive male+female condoms (n=99) or male condoms only (n=50). Baseline: 86% HIV positive; 34% had ≥1 STI.
- **Setting:** Urban Harare, Zimbabwe.
- **Intervention:** Improved access to both male and female condoms (Group A).
- **Comparator:** Access to male condoms only (Group B).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Consistent male condom use with clients at 2 weeks (Group A: 0%→52%; Group B: 0%→82%)
  - Consistent female condom use with clients (Group A: 3%–9% across visits)
  - STI incidence (overall)
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** Approximately 3 months per participant
- **Key findings:**
  - Simple improved access to condoms increased protected sex with clients in both groups.
  - Condom use with boyfriends remained lower than with clients throughout follow-up.
  - STI incidence remained high (16 episodes per 100 woman-months).
  - High self-reported acceptability of female condoms, but client distrust of unfamiliar methods was a barrier.
- **Notes:** Randomized allocation; high HIV/STI burden at baseline; outcomes largely self-reported, with some STI incidence data.

**Study 15** — Acceptability of a microenterprise intervention among female sex workers in Chennai, India
- **Population:** 100 female sex workers in Chennai (median age 35; 61% married; average two children).
- **Setting:** Chennai, India.
- **Intervention:** Microenterprise intervention: tailoring canvas bags (Pi pilot).
- **Comparator:** Control arm (no microenterprise during study).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Number of paying clients per month (follow-up vs control)
  - Monthly income (follow-up vs control)
  - Number of sex partners (follow-up vs control)
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** RCT (pilot)
- **Follow-up:** 6 months
- **Key findings:**
  - Intervention participants reported significantly fewer paying clients per month at follow-up (multivariate regression with bootstrapping).
  - Intervention participants reported increased income and fewer sex partners at follow-up.
  - 75% produced at least one sellable bag by graduation; 60% continued bag production 6 months post-study.
- **Notes:** Small pilot with behavioral and economic outcomes; significance reported without exact p-values in abstract.

**Study 26** — Syndromic management of sexually‐transmitted infections and behavior change interventions on transmission of HIV‐1 in rural Uganda: a community randomised trial
- **Population:** All adults living in 18 rural Ugandan communities (community-randomised).
- **Setting:** Rural Uganda; community-level interventions.
- **Intervention:** Group A: behavioral interventions; Group B: behavioral + STI interventions; Group C: routine government health services and community development activities.
- **Comparator:** Primary comparisons: Group A vs Group C; Group B vs Group C.
- **Outcomes:**
  - HIV-1 incidence (A vs C): RR 0.94 (95% CI 0.6–1.45); p=0.72
  - HIV-1 incidence (B vs C): RR 1.0 (95% CI 0.63–1.58); p=0.98
  - Condom use with last casual partner (prevalence ratio, A vs C): RR 1.12 (95% CI 0.99–1.25)
  - Condom use with last casual partner (prevalence ratio, B vs C): RR 1.27 (95% CI 1.02–1.56)
  - HSV-2 incidence (A vs C): RR 0.65 (95% CI 0.53–0.8)
  - Active syphilis incidence (B vs C): RR 0.52 (95% CI 0.27–0.98)
  - Gonorrhoea prevalence (B vs C): RR 0.25 (95% CI 0.1–0.64)
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** community cluster RCT (analysis per protocol)
- **Follow-up:** Not specified in abstract snippet
- **Key findings:**
  - No reduction in HIV-1 incidence with behavioral interventions alone (A) or combined with STI interventions (B).
  - Condom use with last casual partner increased, significantly so in group B vs C (PR 1.27; 95% CI 1.02–1.56).
  - Reductions in HSV-2 incidence (A vs C), active syphilis incidence (B vs C), and gonorrhoea prevalence (B vs C).
  - Interpretation: interventions insufficient to reduce HIV-1 incidence in mature epidemic context; need more effective strategies.
- **Notes:** Cluster-level randomization; per-protocol analysis; multiple STI biomarkers; not specific to sex workers but includes high-risk populations.

**Study 67** — Promotion of condom use in a high‐risk setting in Nicaragua: a randomised controlled trial
- **Population:** Couples attending 19 motels in Managua, Nicaragua over 24 days (n=6463 couples). Eleven motels mainly used by sex workers and clients; eight by non-commercial sex.
- **Setting:** Managua, Nicaragua motels; high-risk setting for commercial and non-commercial sex.
- **Intervention:** Factorial: (1) Condoms on request vs available in rooms vs handed directly to couples; (2) Health-education material present vs absent in rooms.
- **Comparator:** Varied per factorial contrast (e.g., availability in rooms vs on request; handing directly vs on request; material vs no material).
- **Outcomes:**
  - Condom use for commercial sex: health-education material present vs absent: OR 0.89 (95% CI 0.84–0.94)
  - Condom use for non-commercial sex: health-education material present vs absent: OR 1.03 (95% CI 0.97–1.08)
  - Condom use for commercial sex: condoms available in rooms vs on request: OR 1.31 (95% CI 1.09–1.75)
  - Condom use for non-commercial sex: condoms available in rooms vs on request: OR 1.81 (95% CI 1.14–2.81)
  - Condom use for commercial sex: handing condoms to couples vs on request: OR 1.32 (95% CI 1.03–1.61)
  - Condom use for non-commercial sex: handing condoms to couples vs on request: OR 1.52 (95% CI 1.01–2.38)
- **Adverse events:** Not reported
- **Study design:** cluster RCT (randomized by motel; factorial design)
- **Follow-up:** 24 days (direct room inspection post-visit for used condoms)
- **Key findings:**
  - Condom use was more frequent for commercial than non-commercial sex (60.5% vs 20.2%).
  - Health-education material reduced condom use for commercial sex (OR 0.89; 95% CI 0.84–0.94) and had no effect for non-commercial sex.
  - Making condoms available in rooms increased condom use for both commercial (OR 1.31; 95% CI 1.09–1.75) and non-commercial sex (OR 1.81; 95% CI 1.14–2.81).
  - Directly handing condoms to couples increased use for commercial sex (OR 1.32; 95% CI 1.03–1.61) and was less effective for non-commercial sex (OR 1.52; 95% CI 1.01–2.38) than in-room availability.
  - In-room condom availability was the most effective strategy.
- **Notes:** Objective behavioral outcome (retrieved used condoms). High external validity for motels as key locations; includes establishments primarily visited by sex workers and clients.


## 5. Synthesis & Conclusions
HIV incidence/prevalence: Across studies involving sex workers and high-risk settings, evidence for reducing HIV incidence is limited. In Mexico [8], a brief behavioral intervention reduced HIV incidence density over ~6 months (0 vs 2.01 per 100 person-years), alongside significant declines in combined STI incidence density. However, a large community cluster trial in Uganda [26] observed no reduction in HIV-1 incidence with behavioral interventions alone (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.60–1.45) or combined with STI services (RR 1.00; 0.63–1.58). No trials reported HIV prevalence reductions among sex workers over longer horizons. Overall, HIV incidence findings remain sparse and context-dependent.

STI outcomes: Multiple studies reported biomarker-confirmed STI improvements. In Madagascar [2], adding clinic-based counseling to peer education reduced aggregate STI prevalence (OR 0.7; 95% CI 0.5–0.9), with consistent directions for chlamydia and gonorrhoea (ORs ~0.7). In China [7], culturally adapted VCT reduced any STD at follow-up (OR 0.44; 95% CI 0.24–0.80). In Mexico [8], cumulative STI incidence declined (P=0.049) and combined STI incidence density fell to 13.8 vs 24.92 per 100 person-years (P=0.034). In Uganda [26], although HIV incidence was unchanged, favorable effects were observed for HSV-2 incidence (RR 0.65; 0.53–0.80; behavioral arm), active syphilis incidence (RR 0.52; 0.27–0.98; combined arm), and gonorrhoea prevalence (RR 0.25; 0.10–0.64; combined arm). By contrast, a 12-month RCT in Madagascar [6] found no significant differences in aggregated STI prevalence, potentially reflecting high prior condom promotion and contextual factors. In Thailand [3], STD incidence rates were 24% lower with the option of female condoms but not statistically significant (2.81 vs 3.69 per 100 person-weeks; P=0.18). Overall, evidence supports that targeted behavioral/skills-based counseling and select structural elements can reduce non-HIV STI burden among sex workers, though effects may vary by context, baseline prevention saturation, and power.

Condom use: Several trials showed improvements in protected sex. Madagascar [2] reported higher overall condom use with clients in the peer+clinic arm (OR 1.4; 95% CI 1.1–1.8), with effects strengthening across follow-up. In China [7], consistent condom use with clients increased (OR 2.23; 95% CI 1.26–3.96). Armenia [12] found higher consistent condom use over 6 months (P=0.004) and improved application skills. In Zimbabwe [14], access to male and female condoms increased protected sex with clients in both groups; male condom consistency rose more in the male-only group (0%→82% vs 0%→52% by 2 weeks), while the male+female group showed modest female condom uptake (3%–9%). In Thailand [3], permitting female condoms yielded fewer unprotected acts (5.9% vs 7.1%; P=0.16), though effects were not significant. A large motel-based structural cRCT in Nicaragua [67] showed that in-room condom availability increased condom use for commercial sex (OR 1.31; 95% CI 1.09–1.75) and non-commercial sex (OR 1.81; 1.14–2.81), whereas health-education materials alone were ineffective or even reduced use for commercial sex (OR 0.89; 0.84–0.94). Mexico [8] documented increases in protected acts and declines in unprotected acts (P<0.05). South Africa [10] reported reductions in unprotected sex at 1 month. Collectively, skills-based counseling and structural access (especially convenient condom availability) reliably increase protected sex, though magnitudes vary and ceiling effects can attenuate differences.

Other HIV-related outcomes: Interventions often improved proximal determinants of risk. Knowledge gains were marked in China [7] (p<0.0001). The Woman-Focused program in South Africa [10] reduced daily alcohol/cocaine use and reported victimization at 1 month, addressing key co-factors of HIV risk. A microenterprise pilot in India [15] reduced the number of paying clients and increased income, suggesting potential to lower exposure by addressing economic vulnerability. These upstream changes are promising but are supported by smaller studies, short follow-up, and predominantly self-reported measures.

Heterogeneity: Interventions spanned individual-level counseling (peer+clinic risk-reduction [2], adapted VCT [7], brief behavioral sessions [8,12]), device-choice strategies (adding female condoms [3,14]), structural access (in-room condoms [67]), community-wide packages [26], and economic empowerment [15]. Settings covered Africa (Madagascar [2,6], South Africa [10], Zimbabwe [14], Uganda [26]), Asia (Thailand [3], China [7], Armenia [12], India [15]), and Latin America (Mexico [8], Nicaragua [67]). Populations varied (street-based vs establishment-based FSWs; motel-based commercial sex), baseline prevention exposure, and epidemic context. Outcomes mixed objective biomarkers and self-reports, with follow-up ranging from 24 days to 12 months.

Overall interpretation: There is consistent evidence that behavioral counseling and structural condom access increase condom use among sex workers and clients, and multiple trials demonstrate reductions in non-HIV STIs. Evidence for reducing HIV incidence is limited and context-specific, with one positive RCT signal in a short-term trial [8] and null effects at community scale [26]. Multi-component strategies that combine skills-based counseling, sustained and convenient condom availability, and structural supports (e.g., addressing substance use, violence, economic vulnerability) appear most promising for risk reduction and may be needed to achieve durable impacts on HIV transmission.

## 6. Quality Assessment
- **Study 2:** Quality rating — high
  - Strength: Large individually randomized RCT (n=1000)
  - Strength: Objective STI biomarkers plus behavioral outcomes
  - Strength: Adjusted analyses; consistent direction of effects
  - Strength: Follow-up assessments at multiple time points (2, 4, 6 months)
  - Limitation: Self-reported condom use subject to social desirability bias
  - Limitation: Follow-up limited to 6 months (sustainability unknown)
  - Bias concern: Performance bias possible (behavioral interventions not blindable)
  - Bias concern: Self-report for behavior outcomes
  - Applicability: direct

**Overall evidence strength:** moderate

**Key limitations:**
- Heterogeneity of interventions, settings, and populations limits direct comparability and meta-analysis
- Sparse HIV incidence data and short follow-up windows constrain conclusions on HIV transmission impact
- Frequent reliance on self-reported behaviors introduces potential social desirability and recall bias
- Several trials are small or pilot studies with imprecision and attrition
- Non-randomized designs included (e.g., quasi-experimental) with potential selection/residual confounding
- Incomplete reporting (e.g., missing effect sizes, unclear cluster adjustments, per-protocol analyses)
- Indirectness in some studies (e.g., community-wide interventions not specific to sex workers; mixed client bases)
- Potential ceiling effects in contexts with very high baseline condom use
- Sustainability beyond 6–12 months is largely unmeasured
