# Systematic Review Report

## 1. Review Summary
- **Title:** Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis for opportunistic infections in children with HIV infection
- **Objective:** To assess the effects of routinely administered cotrimoxazole on death and illness episodes in children with HIV infection, and in infants of HIV‐infected mothers.

## 2. Search Results
- Number of candidate studies found: 10

## 3. Screening Results
- Included studies: 1
- Excluded studies: 9
- Decisions:
  - Study 1: INCLUDE — Randomised placebo-controlled trial of daily co-trimoxazole versus placebo in children aged 1-14 years with HIV infection. Meets all criteria: RCT design, intervention cotrimoxazole, comparator placebo, population children <15 years with HIV.
  - Study 2: EXCLUDE — Population consists of HIV-infected adults, not children. Does not meet age criteria.
  - Study 3: EXCLUDE — Population is AIDS patients with Kaposi's sarcoma; no mention of children. Likely adults; does not meet child/infant criteria.
  - Study 4: EXCLUDE — Not a randomised or quasi-randomised trial; case series of four adult men. Study design and population do not meet criteria.
  - Study 5: EXCLUDE — Not a trial; post-mortem observational study. Does not meet study design criterion.
  - Study 6: EXCLUDE — Not a trial; prospective cohort study without an intervention of cotrimoxazole. Does not meet study design criterion.
  - Study 7: EXCLUDE — Not a trial; observational follow-up study. No cotrimoxazole intervention.
  - Study 8: EXCLUDE — Population is HIV-infected adults (median age 32 years) with tuberculosis. Does not meet child/infant criteria.
  - Study 9: EXCLUDE — Not a trial; likely a review or commentary. No original trial data of cotrimoxazole.
  - Study 10: EXCLUDE — Not a trial; prospective observational study of pneumonia incidence. No randomisation to cotrimoxazole.

## 4. Evidence Extraction Summary
**Study 1** — Chintu et al., 2004
- **Population:** Children aged 1-14 years with clinical features of HIV infection in Zambia; 534 children (after exclusion of 7 HIV-negative) randomized; median follow-up 19 months.
- **Setting:** Zambia, resource-poor setting with high background bacterial resistance to cotrimoxazole.
- **Intervention:** Daily cotrimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) prophylaxis; dose not specified in abstract; duration: throughout follow-up (median 19 months).
- **Comparator:** Placebo.
- **Outcomes:**
  - All-cause mortality: HR 0.57 (95% CI 0.43–0.77); p=0.0002
  - Mortality beyond 12 months follow-up: HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.27–0.84)
  - Neutropenia (neutrophil count <0.5x10^9/L): RR 2.29; p=0.06
- **Adverse events:** Grade 3 or 4 adverse events: 16/267 (6%) in cotrimoxazole group vs 18/267 (7%) in placebo group; rash (1 in placebo); neutropenia (neutrophils <0.5x10^9/L) on at least one occasion: 16/267 (6%) cotrimoxazole vs 7/267 (3%) placebo, p=0.06.
- **Study design:** Double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial (RCT)
- **Follow-up:** Median 19 months
- **Key findings:**
  - Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis significantly reduced all-cause mortality by 43% (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.43–0.77, p=0.0002).
  - The mortality benefit was consistent across children followed beyond 12 months (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.84).
  - No significant heterogeneity of effect across age groups (p=0.82) or baseline CD4 counts (p=0.36).
  - Adverse events were similar between groups, with a trend toward higher neutropenia with cotrimoxazole (p=0.06).
  - Only 1 case of Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia identified (in placebo group).
  - Trial stopped early (October 2003) due to clear benefit.
- **Notes:** Only one study was provided. The CHAP trial is a landmark study supporting cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in HIV-infected children in resource-limited settings. Early stopping may overestimate treatment effect. Dose regimen not detailed in abstract; typically trimethoprim 5 mg/kg + sulfamethoxazole 25 mg/kg daily.


## 5. Synthesis & Conclusions
This systematic review identified only one included study, the Children with HIV Antibiotic Prophylaxis (CHAP) trial (Chintu et al., 2004) [1], a double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial conducted in Zambia among 534 HIV-infected children aged 1–14 years. The trial provides direct evidence on the effects of daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis on mortality but limited information on illness episodes. No studies were identified that assessed cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in infants of HIV-infected mothers.

Effects on death:
Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis was associated with a statistically significant 43% reduction in all-cause mortality compared to placebo (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.43–0.77, p=0.0002) [1]. The mortality benefit persisted and remained significant for children followed beyond 12 months (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.84) [1]. Subgroup analyses showed no significant heterogeneity of effect by age (p=0.82) or baseline CD4 count (p=0.36), suggesting a consistent benefit across the enrolled paediatric population [1]. However, the trial was stopped early because of clear benefit, which may overestimate the magnitude of the true mortality reduction.

Effects on illness episodes:
The CHAP trial reported that only one case of Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia was identified in the placebo group and none in the cotrimoxazole group; however, data on other specific illness episodes (e.g., bacterial infections, diarrhoea) were not extracted [1]. Grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in similar proportions: 6% in the cotrimoxazole group vs 7% in placebo [1]. Neutropenia (neutrophil count <0.5×10⁹/L) on at least one occasion was more common with cotrimoxazole (6% vs 3%, RR 2.29, p=0.06), although this difference was not statistically significant at the conventional 5% level and the confidence interval was not reported, limiting interpretation [1].

Evidence gaps:
The most critical gap is the absence of studies addressing cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in infants of HIV-infected mothers. The CHAP trial included children aged 1–14 years, so the evidence is not directly applicable to infants under 1 year who might benefit from prophylaxis during the breastfeeding period or before definitive HIV diagnosis. Furthermore, the single-study evidence base means that the findings cannot be externally validated. The early stopping of the trial, although ethically justified, introduces uncertainty about the true effect size. Data on illness episodes beyond Pneumocystis pneumonia are sparse, and the safety assessment is limited by the relatively small sample size and short follow-up.

Overall evidence assessment:
The evidence from one moderate-quality RCT suggests that daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis reduces all-cause mortality in HIV-infected children aged 1–14 years in resource-limited settings, with an acceptable short-term safety profile. However, confidence in this finding is limited by the single-study design, early stopping, and unclear risk of bias in some domains. There is insufficient evidence to determine the effects of cotrimoxazole on illness episodes in this population, and no evidence for infants of HIV-infected mothers.

## 6. Quality Assessment
- **Study 1:** Quality rating — moderate
  - Strength: Double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial design (low risk of performance and detection bias).
  - Strength: Statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.43-0.77, p=0.0002).
  - Strength: Consistent mortality benefit beyond 12 months follow-up (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27-0.84).
  - Strength: No significant heterogeneity of effect across age groups (p=0.82) or baseline CD4 counts (p=0.36).
  - Strength: Adequate follow-up period (median 19 months).
  - Strength: Conducted in a high bacterial resistance setting, enhancing generalizability to similar resource-limited contexts.
  - Limitation: Trial stopped early due to clear benefit (October 2003), which may overestimate the true treatment effect.
  - Limitation: Randomisation method and allocation concealment not described in detail.
  - Limitation: Only one study contributed evidence; no replication in other settings or populations.
  - Limitation: No infants of HIV-infected mothers or children <1 year of age were included—evidence gap for the 'infants of HIV-infected mothers' part of the review question.
  - Limitation: Sample size (534 children) limits power for secondary outcomes and adverse events; neutropenia risk estimate (RR 2.29, p=0.06) has low precision (no CI reported).
  - Limitation: Cotrimoxazole dose not specified in abstract; exact dosing regimen unclear.
  - Limitation: Outcome data on illness episodes besides Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia not explicitly reported.
  - Limitation: Limited detail on completeness of follow-up and handling of missing data.
  - Bias concern: Early stopping for benefit: interim analysis revealed clear benefit, leading to early termination (October 2003), which may lead to an overestimation of the treatment effect (stopping bias).
  - Bias concern: Lack of description of randomisation procedure and allocation concealment prevents assessment of selection bias.
  - Bias concern: Incomplete reporting of safety outcomes (CI for neutropenia RR not provided).
  - Bias concern: Potential selective outcome reporting: only mortality and neutropenia were detailed; other illness episodes not individually reported.
  - Applicability: indirect

**Overall evidence strength:** moderate

**Key limitations:**
- Single trial (CHAP trial) provides the entire evidence base; no replication in other settings or populations.
- Trial stopped early for benefit, potentially overestimating treatment effect.
- No studies identified for infants of HIV-infected mothers.
- Limited data on illness episodes; only mortality and neutropenia were extractable.
- Unclear risk of selection bias due to incomplete reporting of randomisation method and allocation concealment.
- Sample size limits precision and power for safety outcomes; CI for neutropenia risk not reported.
- Cotrimoxazole dose and exact formulation not specified in the extracted data.
- Generalisability may be limited to resource-limited settings with high background bacterial resistance; applicability to settings with low antimicrobial resistance or high access to antiretroviral therapy is uncertain.
