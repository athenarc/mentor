## 5. Synthesis & Conclusions
The CHAP trial provides high-quality evidence supporting cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in HIV-infected children in resource-limited settings. In a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 534 HIV-infected children (aged 1-14 years) in Zambia, daily cotrimoxazole significantly reduced mortality compared with placebo (28% vs 42%; hazard ratio 0.57, 95% CI 0.43–0.77; p=0.0002) [1]. The benefit was consistent across all ages (heterogeneity p=0.82), baseline CD4 counts (heterogeneity p=0.36), and in children followed beyond 12 months (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.84; heterogeneity p=0.60) [1].Safety outcomes were favorable: grade 3–4 adverse events occurred in 6% of the cotrimoxazole group versus 7% in the placebo group (p>0.05), with no significant increase in neutropenia (p=0.06) [1]. Only one case of Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (PCP) was identified by immunofluorescence among 73 pneumonia aspirates, and it occurred in the placebo group—suggesting that other pathogens contributed substantially to respiratory morbidity [1].Limitations include the use of clinical (rather than laboratory-confirmed) HIV diagnosis for some participants and the single-site setting in Zambia, which may limit generalizability to other geographic or healthcare contexts. Despite early trial stopping for benefit, the magnitude of effect, narrow confidence interval, and consistency across subgroups support a robust mortality benefit. No relevant studies were identified that reported conflicting findings in similar populations. The evidence strength for mortality reduction is strong, but evidence for specific infection prevention (e.g., PCP) remains limited due to sparse pathogen data.

**Key limitations:**

- HIV status determined by clinical staging for some participants instead of serological testing

- Limited power to detect differences in rare adverse events such as neutropenia
