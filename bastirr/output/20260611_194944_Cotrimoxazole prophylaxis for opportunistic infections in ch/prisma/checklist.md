| INTRODUCTION | 3. Rationale | Describe the rationale for the review in the context of existing knowledge. | The majority of children with HIV infection live in low‐income countries without access to antiretroviral drugs. The prevention and early treatment of opportunistic infections are the mainstay of their medical management. Cotrimoxazole is cheap and effective against a wide range of organisms, including Pneumocystis jiroveci pneumonia (PCP), which is an important cause of death and illness in the first year of life. It is safe with relatively few side effects. Diagnosis of HIV in children is complicated by the presence of maternal antibodies in early life. Providing prophylaxis based initially on maternal status is one possible solution. However, routine prophylactic treatment is difficult to deliver in low‐resource settings, and could also lead to increased resistance to the drug. |

If comparing groups, describe the direction of the effect. | Study characteristics: One double-blind, individually randomized, placebo-controlled trial from Zambia evaluated daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in children aged 1–14 years with clinical features of HIV infection over a median 19 months of follow-up [1]. Seven children later found HIV-negative were excluded; an intention-to-treat approach was stated. The regimen was daily cotrimoxazole; dosing and start timing were not specified in the abstract. The comparator was placebo.  All-cause mortality: Cotrimoxazole significantly reduced mortality versus placebo (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.43–0.77; p=0.0002) [1]. The benefit appeared sustained beyond 12 months (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.84) [1]. Within-study subgroup analyses found no heterogeneity by age or baseline CD4 count [1]. Precision for the primary effect was good, with confidence intervals clearly excluding no effect.  Illness episodes (PCP and serious bacterial infections): The abstract did not provide comparative counts for pneumonia or serious bacterial infections. Among pneumonia cases sampled, Pneumocystis jiroveci was identified in 1/73 nasopharyngeal aspirates from the placebo arm [1], suggesting low detection in tested samples, but this does not permit comparative efficacy estimates for PCP or serious bacterial infections.  Hospitalizations: Hospitalization outcomes were not reported in the abstract, precluding conclusions about impacts on inpatient care.  Adverse events: Overall grade 3/4 adverse events were similar between groups (6% vs 7%) [1]. Severe neutropenia (<0.5×10^9/L at least once) occurred more often in the cotrimoxazole arm (6% vs 3%), with borderline statistical significance (p=0.06) [1]. Rash was rare and reported once in the placebo arm [1]. These data suggest acceptable safety with possible increased risk of severe neutropenia requiring monitoring. Follow-up was a median of 19 months [1]. No trials of cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in HIV-exposed but uninfected infants were included in the extracted evidence, so effects in that population remain uncertain here. Morbidity, hospitalization, and resistance outcomes were insufficiently reported to assess consistency or precision across these domains.
