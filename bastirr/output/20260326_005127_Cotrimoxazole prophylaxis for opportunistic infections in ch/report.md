## 4. Evidence Extraction Summary
**Study 1** — Kazembe et al. 2004 (CHAP trial)
- **Population:** Children aged 1-14 years with clinical features of HIV infection in Zambia. 541 children were randomly assigned; 7 were identified as HIV negative and excluded, leaving 534 HIV-infected children included in the analysis.
- **Setting:** Zambia - resource-poor setting with high levels of bacterial resistance to cotrimoxazole
- **Intervention:** Daily co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) prophylaxis
- **Comparator:** Placebo (double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial)
- **Outcomes:**
  - All-cause mortality (primary outcome): HR 0.57 (95% CI 0.43–0.77); p=0.0002
  - Mortality beyond 12 months follow-up: HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.27–0.84)
  - Grade 3 or 4 adverse events
  - Neutropenia (neutrophil count <0.5x10^9/L); p=0.06
- **Adverse events:** 16 (6%) children in co-trimoxazole group had grade 3 or 4 adverse events vs 18 (7%) in placebo group. Events included rash (one in placebo group) and neutropenia (neutrophil count <0.5x10^9/L on one occasion: 16 [6%] co-trimoxazole vs 7 [3%] placebo, p=0.06).
- **Study design:** RCT
- **Follow-up:** Median 19 months. Trial stopped early in October 2003 by data and safety monitoring committee due to clear benefit.
- **Key findings:**
  - Co-trimoxazole significantly reduced mortality by 43% (HR 0.57 [95% CI 0.43-0.77], p=0.0002)
  - 74 (28%) children died in co-trimoxazole group vs 112 (42%) in placebo group
  - Benefit was consistent across children followed beyond 12 months (HR 0.48 [0.27-0.84])
  - Benefit was consistent across all ages (test for heterogeneity p=0.82)
  - Benefit was consistent across all baseline CD4 counts (test for heterogeneity p=0.36)
  - No significant difference in grade 3 or 4 adverse events between groups (6% vs 7%)
  - Slightly more neutropenia with co-trimoxazole (6% vs 3%, p=0.06)
  - Only 1 case of Pneumocystis carinii identified (in placebo group) among 73 nasopharyngeal aspirates
  - Trial was stopped early due to overwhelming evidence of benefit
  - Recommendation: Children of all ages with HIV clinical features should receive co-trimoxazole prophylaxis in resource-poor settings regardless of local resistance patterns
- **Notes:** This landmark RCT demonstrated that co-trimoxazole prophylaxis significantly reduces mortality in HIV-infected children even in settings with high bacterial resistance. The trial was stopped early for efficacy. Analysis was by intention to treat.


## 5. Synthesis & Conclusions
The systematic review identified one high-quality randomized controlled trial (the CHAP trial) addressing the effects of cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in African children with HIV infection. This double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial by Kazembe et al. [1] enrolled 534 HIV-infected children aged 1-14 years in Zambia and demonstrated substantial mortality benefit with cotrimoxazole prophylaxis.

Regarding mortality outcomes, cotrimoxazole prophylaxis significantly reduced all-cause mortality by 43% (Hazard Ratio 0.57, 95% CI 0.43-0.77, p=0.0002) [1]. In the intervention group, 28% (74/267) of children died compared to 42% (112/267) in the placebo group. The survival benefit persisted beyond 12 months of follow-up, with a mortality hazard ratio of 0.48 (95% CI 0.27-0.84) among children followed longer [1].

Heterogeneity analyses confirmed that the mortality benefit was consistent across all age groups (test for heterogeneity p=0.82) and across all baseline CD4 count categories (test for heterogeneity p=0.36), suggesting the intervention is effective regardless of disease severity or age [1].

Regarding safety, no significant difference was observed in grade 3-4 adverse events between groups (6% in treatment vs 7% in placebo) [1]. A borderline increase in neutropenia (neutrophil count <0.5×10^9/L) was noted in the cotrimoxazole group (6% vs 3%, p=0.06), but this did not translate to clinically significant hematologic complications [1]. The study identified only 1 case of Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (in the placebo group) among 73 nasopharyngeal aspirates examined [1].

The trial was conducted in a resource-poor setting with high local bacterial resistance to cotrimoxazole, demonstrating that prophylaxis remains effective even in contexts where antibiotic resistance is common [1]. Due to the overwhelming mortality benefit observed, the trial was stopped early in October 2003 by an independent data and safety monitoring committee [1].

## 6. Quality Assessment
- **Study 1:** Quality rating — high
  - Strength: Double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial design (highest level of evidence)
  - Strength: Large sample size (534 HIV-infected children analyzed) with adequate statistical power
  - Strength: Independent data and safety monitoring committee with prespecified stopping rules
  - Strength: Intention-to-treat analysis properly conducted
  - Strength: Comprehensive adverse event monitoring and reporting
  - Strength: Heterogeneity testing showed consistent effects across age groups and CD4 counts
  - Strength: Trial stopped early for overwhelming mortality benefit, confirming strong effect size
  - Limitation: Single-center study conducted in Zambia may limit generalizability to other geographic regions
  - Limitation: Trial stopped early potentially limiting ability to assess longer-term outcomes
  - Limitation: Population limited to children aged 1-14 years (infants under 1 year not included in this study)
  - Limitation: Setting had high bacterial resistance to cotrimoxazole, which may not represent all resource-limited contexts
  - Bias concern: Stopped early for efficacy - some longer-term outcomes not fully assessed
  - Bias concern: Single geographic setting limits external validity
  - Bias concern: No reported information on allocation concealment methods
  - Applicability: direct

**Overall evidence strength:** strong

**Key limitations:**
- Only one study identified for this review - limited ability to synthesize across studies or conduct meta-analysis
- Study population did not include infants under 1 year of age, representing a gap in evidence for this important group
- Evidence limited to single geographic region (Zambia), requiring consideration of applicability to other settings
- Trial stopped early for efficacy - longer-term safety and effectiveness data beyond 19 months follow-up not available
- No assessment of impact on specific opportunistic infections beyond Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia
- Limited data on illness episodes (morbidity) - focus was primarily on mortality outcome
