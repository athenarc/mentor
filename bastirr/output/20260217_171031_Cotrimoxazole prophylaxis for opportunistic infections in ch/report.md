1. Review Summary
- Title: Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis for opportunistic infections in children with HIV infection
- Objective: To assess the effects of routinely administered cotrimoxazole on death and illness episodes in children with HIV infection, and in infants of HIV‐infected mothers.

4. Evidence Extraction Summary (key PICO elements per included study)
- Study [1]
  Population: Children aged 1–14 years with clinical features of HIV infection in Zambia.
  Intervention: Daily co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) prophylaxis.
  Comparator: Placebo.
  Follow-up: Median 19 months; subgroup analysis ≥12 months reported.
  Outcomes:
    • All-cause mortality (overall): HR 0.57 (95% CI 0.43–0.77); p=0.0002
    • All-cause mortality (≥12 months subgroup): HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.27–0.84)
  Adverse events:
    • Grade 3–4 adverse events (overall): 16 (6%) vs 18 (7%)
    • Neutrophil count <0.5×10^9/L (on one occasion): 16 (6%) vs 7 (3%); p=0.06
    • Rash: 0 vs 1 case
  Notes: Double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial (intention-to-treat). Stopped early (Oct 2003) for benefit. Randomized n=541; 7 later identified as HIV-negative and excluded. Benefit consistent across ages and baseline CD4; effect present beyond 12 months. Very low PCP detection in NPAs despite high local bacterial resistance.
- Approximate absolute effect on mortality: 28% vs 42% (ARR 14.0 percentage points); NNT ≈ 7.1 (~7) over ~19 months.

5. Synthesis & Conclusions (Narrative synthesis)
- Quality evidence from one pediatric randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (CHAP, Zambia) shows that daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis substantially reduces all-cause mortality among HIV-infected children aged 1–14 years compared with placebo (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.43–0.77).
- The mortality benefit persisted among those followed ≥12 months (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.84) and did not vary meaningfully by age or baseline CD4 count, suggesting broad applicability across pediatric subgroups with clinical features of HIV.
- Serious adverse events were similar in cotrimoxazole and placebo groups (6% vs 7%), with a nonsignificant trend to more transient neutropenia in the cotrimoxazole arm (6% vs 3%, p=0.06) and very few rashes.
- Despite high local bacterial resistance to cotrimoxazole, mortality reduction was observed, indicating that prophylaxis confers benefit that is not negated by resistance patterns in this setting.
- Based on observed risks (28% vs 42%), the absolute risk reduction is about 14 percentage points, corresponding to a number needed to treat of approximately 7 over roughly 19 months.
- Data on illness episodes beyond mortality (e.g., hospitalizations, specific opportunistic infections) were limited in the pediatric RCT abstract; however, observational pediatric data suggest lower PCP incidence among children on prophylaxis. These observational findings were not included in the primary evidence due to study design.
- No randomized evidence was identified for routine cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in HIV-exposed but uninfected infants (based on maternal status), representing an important evidence gap.
- Implications: In resource-limited settings, cotrimoxazole prophylaxis should be considered routine for HIV-infected children, including in areas with high bacterial resistance. Programs should monitor hematologic tolerance and integrate prophylaxis with broader HIV care and nutrition support.

6. Quality Assessment (evidence strength and limitations)
- Risk of bias: Low risk for blinding and randomization (double-blind randomized design; ITT analysis). Allocation concealment cannot be fully assessed from abstract but likely adequate given design; outcome ascertainment (mortality) is objective.
- Early stopping: The trial was stopped early for benefit, which can inflate effect estimates; this introduces some concern.
- Selective reporting: Mortality and adverse events were predefined primary outcomes; selective reporting risk appears low based on abstract.
- Attrition: Not explicitly detailed in abstract; intention-to-treat analysis was used, reducing attrition bias concern.
- Imprecision and inconsistency: The mortality effect is precise (95% CI does not cross 1); only one pediatric RCT is available, so between-study inconsistency cannot be assessed.
- Indirectness: Direct evidence for HIV-infected children 1–14 years. No RCT evidence for HIV-exposed uninfected infants; extrapolation to that group would be indirect.
- Publication bias: Not assessable with a single RCT.
- Overall certainty: Moderate to high for mortality reduction in HIV-infected children in similar low-resource settings; downgraded slightly for early stopping and single-study evidence base.
