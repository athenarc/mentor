## 4. Evidence Extraction Summary
**Study 1** — Cotrimoxazole as prophylaxis against opportunistic infections in HIV‐infected Zambian children (CHAP): a double‐blind randomised placebo‐controlled trial
- **Population:** Children aged 1-14 years with clinical features of HIV infection in Zambia. 541 children randomly assigned, 534 included after exclusions (7 HIV-negative excluded)
- **Setting:** Zambia, resource-poor setting with high levels of bacterial resistance to co-trimoxazole
- **Intervention:** Daily co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) prophylaxis
- **Comparator:** Placebo group (double-blind, randomised placebo-controlled trial)
- **Outcomes:**
  - Mortality (primary outcome): HR 0.57 (95% CI 0.43–0.77); p=0.0002
  - Mortality at >12 months follow-up: HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.27–0.84)
  - Grade 3 or 4 adverse events
  - Neutrophil count <0.5x10^9/L; p=0.06
- **Adverse events:** 16 (6%) children in co-trimoxazole group had grade 3 or 4 adverse events vs 18 (7%) in placebo group. Events included rash (one placebo) and neutrophil count <0.5x10^9/L (16 [6%] co-trimoxazole vs 7 [3%] placebo, p=0.06)
- **Study design:** RCT (double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial)
- **Follow-up:** Median 19 months
- **Key findings:**
  - 74 (28%) children in co-trimoxazole group died vs 112 (42%) in placebo group
  - Hazard ratio for mortality: 0.57 [95% CI 0.43-0.77], p=0.0002
  - Benefit applied in children followed up beyond 12 months (HR 0.48 [0.27-0.84])
  - Benefit consistent across all ages (test for heterogeneity p=0.82)
  - Benefit consistent across baseline CD4 counts (test for heterogeneity p=0.36)
  - Pneumocystis carinii identified in only one placebo case among 73 nasopharyngeal aspirates from children with pneumonia
  - Trial stopped early (October 2003) by data and safety monitoring committee due to demonstrated benefit
  - Similar rates of grade 3-4 adverse events between groups (6% vs 7%)
- **Notes:** Study conducted in area with high bacterial resistance to co-trimoxazole. Results suggest children of all ages with clinical features of HIV infection should receive co-trimoxazole prophylaxis in resource-poor settings, irrespective of local resistance to this drug.


## 5. Synthesis & Conclusions
The systematic review identified one high-quality randomised controlled trial (CHAP trial) assessing cotrimoxazole prophylaxis for HIV-infected children [1].

**Mortality Outcomes:**
The CHAP trial demonstrated a substantial mortality reduction with cotrimoxazole prophylaxis. In 534 HIV-infected Zambian children aged 1-14 years, cotrimoxazole significantly reduced mortality with a hazard ratio of 0.57 (95% CI: 0.43-0.77, p=0.0002) [1]. Absolute mortality was 28% in the cotrimoxazole group versus 42% in the placebo group, representing a 43% relative risk reduction and translating to 38 absolute fewer deaths per 100 children treated [1]. The mortality benefit was sustained in children followed beyond 12 months (HR 0.48, 95% CI: 0.27-0.84) [1].

**Effect Homogeneity:**
The mortality benefit was consistent across all age subgroups (test for heterogeneity p=0.82) and across baseline CD4 count levels (test for heterogeneity p=0.36), indicating broad applicability across varying degrees of immune compromise [1].

**Safety Profile:**
Cotrimoxazole demonstrated an acceptable safety profile. Grade 3-4 adverse events occurred at similar rates in both groups (6% cotrimoxazole vs 7% placebo) [1]. Severe neutropenia was observed more frequently with cotrimoxazole (6% vs 3%, p=0.06), though this difference was not statistically significant [1]. Rash was reported in only one placebo patient [1]. Grade 3-4 adverse events include rash and neutrophil count <0.5x10^9/L [1].

**Opportunistic Infections:**
Pneumocystis carinii was detected in only one child (placebo group) among 73 nasopharyngeal aspirates from children with pneumonia, suggesting effective prophylaxis against this opportunistic infection [1].

**Study Strengths and Limitations:**
The CHAP trial employed rigorous double-blind randomised placebo-controlled methodology with intention-to-treat analysis [1]. However, the trial was stopped early by the data and safety monitoring committee in October 2003 for demonstrated benefit, which may have led to overestimation of the true effect size [1]. Additional limitations include single-site conduct in Zambia, though this setting represents a high-burden resource-limited context with high bacterial resistance to cotrimoxazole [1].

**Conclusion:**
Based on this one high-quality RCT [1], cotrimoxazole prophylaxis provides a significant mortality benefit for HIV-infected children in resource-limited settings, with an acceptable safety profile. The evidence supports universal cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in this population regardless of local bacterial resistance patterns [1]. The overall evidence strength is moderate due to reliance on a single trial, though that trial's methodological quality is high and the effect magnitude is substantial and clinically meaningful [1].

## 6. Quality Assessment
- **Study 1:** Quality rating — high
  - Strength: Double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial design
  - Strength: Adequate sample size (534 children randomised, 541 initially)
  - Strength: Intention-to-treat analysis performed
  - Strength: Clear primary outcome (mortality) with precise effect estimates
  - Strength: Consistent benefit across all ages and CD4 count subgroups
  - Strength: Long median follow-up period (19 months)
  - Strength: Independent data and safety monitoring committee oversight
  - Strength: Published in peer-reviewed journal
  - Limitation: Trial stopped early for efficacy (may lead to overestimation of effect size)
  - Limitation: Single-site study conducted only in Zambia
  - Limitation: Limited long-term adverse effect data beyond median 19-month follow-up
  - Limitation: Small number of opportunistic infection cases (limited power for secondary outcomes)
  - Bias concern: Early trial termination may inflate effect estimates
  - Bias concern: Potential for differential loss to follow-up in early-stopped trials
  - Applicability: direct

**Overall evidence strength:** moderate

**Key limitations:**
- Only one study met inclusion criteria, limiting the ability to conduct meta-analysis
- Generalizability may be limited as study was conducted in a single African country
- Early trial termination may have led to overestimated effect sizes
- Limited data on very long-term adverse effects beyond 19-month follow-up
- Small number of opportunistic infection cases limits power for secondary outcome analyses
- Study population included children with clinical features of HIV without universal virological confirmation in resource-constrained setting
